Short answer: you look them up in four places — Mexico's federal professional license registry, the specialty board for their field, the health regulator's sanctions list, and the clinic's own accreditation record. None of these are hard to find once you know where to look. The problem is that almost nobody tells patients where "where to look" actually is.

Patient-run communities have taken on part of this work themselves. Facebook groups dedicated to researching providers in Tijuana, Cancún, and Guadalajara have grown into communities of tens of thousands, built around one recurring question: "has anyone used this guy?" These groups serve a real purpose, and the collective experience shared inside them is valuable. They are not, however, a substitute for pulling the actual government record. A recommendation confirms that one patient had a good experience — it does not confirm whether a surgeon's board certification lapsed three years ago, or whether a homonym with the same name carries three sanctions on file.

Why This Actually Matters

Mexico trains excellent physicians. The country has some of the busiest, most experienced cosmetic and bariatric surgery hubs in the world, and plenty of surgeons there hold credentials that would be perfectly at home in Houston or Los Angeles. That's precisely why verification matters — the good providers have nothing to hide, and checking them takes ten minutes and confirms what they've already told you.

The flip side showed up in 2023, when the CDC and Texas health officials traced a fungal meningitis outbreak to two clinics in Matamoros, Mexico. Patients who'd had procedures like liposuction and breast augmentation under epidural anesthesia developed infections from a drug-resistant fungus, Fusarium solani. By the CDC's August 2023 update, the agency had recorded 10 confirmed and 14 probable cases, with 12 deaths among those exposed. It's an extreme case, not the norm — but it's also a real illustration of what happens when a patient can't see behind a clinic's front door before booking.

You don't need a worst-case outbreak to justify checking credentials, though. The far more common problem is quieter: a doctor whose "board certification" expired years ago and was never renewed, or a cosmetic surgeon marketing themselves as a plastic surgeon when those are, legally and educationally, two different things in Mexico.

Mexico does have real oversight in place. Specialty medical boards certify surgeons field by field, and a federal health regulator tracks sanctions against clinics and providers. None of it, however, was built for a patient in Ohio to navigate from a phone the week before a flight — the records live in separate systems, entirely in Spanish, and knowing a system exists is a long way from knowing how to read what it says correctly.

What "Board-Certified" Doesn't Tell You

Here's where a lot of American patients get tripped up. In Mexico, as in the US, "board-certified" is a specific, regulated claim — but a "board-certified cosmetic surgeon" and a "board-certified plastic surgeon" are not the same credential, even though both routinely get marketed with identical language.

A board-certified plastic surgeon has completed a minimum of six years of accredited residency training specifically in plastic, aesthetic, and reconstructive surgery, recognized by Mexico's national specialty certification framework. A "cosmetic surgeon" credential can, in some cases, come from a much shorter training pathway that doesn't include the full surgical residency. Both may call themselves "board-certified" in their marketing. Only one of them has the training to manage a serious surgical complication if one happens on the table.

There's a second layer to this that trips people up just as often: certification in Mexico typically expires and has to be renewed. A surgeon can keep a "board-certified" badge on their website for years after it lapsed, simply because nothing forces the claim to be updated and nothing prompts a patient to ask when it was last renewed.

The Blind Spots a Website Can't Show You

Beyond the certification question, a handful of patterns show up often enough in cosmetic and medical tourism marketing that they're worth naming directly — because none of them are visible from a clinic's website, no matter how polished it looks.

01

The homonym problem

Common surnames create a genuine identification problem. Two different doctors can share a nearly identical name — one with hundreds of glowing reviews, another with a sanction on file — and there's no way to tell them apart without cross-checking against an official record, photo included. Get this wrong and you can walk away feeling confident about a doctor you never actually verified.

02

The slippery clinic identity

Clinic identity is just as slippery. A facility that's been sanctioned can close and reopen under a new name at the same address, with reviews starting fresh at zero. Nothing about a freshly rebranded website looks anything but new and clean, which is exactly the point.

03

The question nobody asks

And then there's the question almost nobody asks until it's too late: what actually happens if a complication requires more care than the clinic itself can provide. Which hospital does the surgeon transfer patients to, how far away is it, and does it have an ICU? A provider with a real answer will give it immediately. One without a plan will get vague — and vague, here, is the whole red flag.
