What a Review Actually Tells You — And What It Doesn't

A five-star review tells you that a past patient was happy with their communication, their recovery suite, or their results. It does not tell you whether the surgeon who performed the procedure has an active license, whether their board certification has lapsed, or whether they've had a formal complaint filed against them.

Reviews are a measure of satisfaction. They are not a measure of credentials. A surgeon can have hundreds of glowing reviews and, separately, an expired specialty certification that nobody checked because nobody thought to ask.

This isn't a hypothetical. It's a pattern that shows up often enough to have a name in the small world of people who actually do this kind of verification work: a doctor's public reputation and their official standing with Mexican regulators can tell two completely different stories.

There's no reason a patient would ever discover the gap between them from a review site. In the United States, a hospital credentials every surgeon before they're allowed to operate, and that vetting is effectively invisible to the patient. In Mexico, that same information exists, but nobody hands it to you. It's scattered across separate government databases, published only in Spanish, and organized in a way that assumes the person searching already knows what they're looking for.

The Two Licenses Most Patients Don't Know Exist

In the U.S., "board certified" is a single, relatively well-understood concept. In Mexico, a physician's credentials are split across at least two separate systems, and understanding the difference matters more than it might seem.

01

Cédula profesional

The baseline medical license. Confirms the physician completed medical school and is authorized to practice medicine in Mexico. Every licensed doctor has one.

02

Cédula de especialidad & CONACEM

Confirms completed specialty residency and whether that specialty training is still considered current. Unlike a base license, CONACEM certification typically expires and must be renewed — often every five years.

The distinction matters because a physician can have a completely valid, active cédula profesional and, at the same time, a specialty certification that expired years ago and was never renewed. Both things can be true simultaneously, and neither fact is visible from a clinic's website or a patient review.

Three Patterns Worth Knowing About

Anyone who spends time cross-referencing these registries starts to notice the same handful of situations repeating. None of them are rare enough to dismiss, and none of them would show up in a normal Google search.

01

The certification that quietly expired

Specialty certification in Mexico isn't permanent — it has to be renewed on a schedule. Some physicians continue listing credentials on their websites and social media that were accurate years ago but haven't been current for some time. Nobody requires them to update the listing, and nobody checks unless someone specifically looks.

02

The clinic that changed its name

A private clinic that has been sanctioned or closed by Mexican health authorities can, in some cases, reopen at the same address under a new name. Reviews start over at zero. Past sanctions don't follow the new name in a casual search. From the outside, it can look like a brand-new, spotless business.

03

The doctor who isn't quite the doctor you found

Common names create real ambiguity. A surgeon with hundreds of positive reviews and a surgeon with a documented sanction can share a nearly identical name. Searching the wrong entry in a registry means confidently verifying the wrong person — which is, in a meaningful sense, worse than not verifying at all, because it creates false confidence.

What You Can Check Yourself, For Free

None of this requires paying anyone. The registries are public. What they require is knowing where to look and being willing to work through them in Spanish.

SELF-VERIFICATION CHECKLIST
✓SEP registry — search by the doctor's full legal name to confirm an active cédula profesional
✓CONACEM — check whether specialty certification is current, and for which specific procedure it applies
✓COFEPRIS — Mexico's federal health regulator publishes sanctions, alerts, and clinic closures
✓Reverse image search — before-and-after photos can be checked against other sources to confirm they belong to the clinic presenting them
Doing this properly, for one doctor and one clinic, realistically takes a few hours the first time — mostly because none of these systems are built for a first-time user, and none of them are in English.

When It's Worth Paying Someone Else to Do It

If you've already narrowed your search to one clinic and you're comfortable navigating Spanish-language government databases, doing it yourself is entirely reasonable, and free.

Where it starts to make more sense to pay for a verification service is when you're comparing more than one surgeon, when your Spanish isn't strong enough to confidently interpret what a registry entry actually means, or when you'd simply rather have a documented, dated report you can reference — and share with family — before you commit thousands of dollars and book a flight.

The cost of getting this wrong isn't abstract. It's the deposit you wired before finding out the certification had lapsed. It's flying to a clinic that closed under a different name once before. Measured against a procedure that costs anywhere from $4,000 to $20,000, spending well under $100 to confirm the basics is a small fraction of the total decision.
